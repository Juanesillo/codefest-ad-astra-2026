BACKGROUND
• X-37B is an experimental reusable spaceplane, similar to the US Space Shuttle but much smaller,
completely robotic, and using more advanced technologies.1
• X-37B is designed to be launched into space on top of a large rocket, stay in orbit for months to
years, and then re-enter and land on a runway. It has thrusters for on-orbit maneuvering and de-
orbit, but no engines for powered flight in the air: it is a glider in the atmosphere.
• X-37B started as a NASA program in 1999, but was transferred to DARPA in 2004. DARPA transferred it
to the US Air Force (USAF) in 2006 after budget issues.2
• Hobbyist tracking indicates the X-37B typically operates at an inclination of 38-54.5 degrees and an
altitude of 285 to 400 km (180 to 250 mi), although the seventh flight was tracked going into a highly
elliptical orbit (HEO) out to 38,838 km.3
• The X-37B is an asset of the US Space Force (USSF)’s 3rd Space Experimentation Squadron, located
at Schriever Space Force Base, Colorado.4 However, as of , the USSF’s Space Delta 9,

DEBATE OVER MISSION AND RATIONALE FOR THE X-37B PROGRAM
Official objectives of the X-37B program include “space experimentation, risk reduction and concept
of operations development for reusable space vehicle technologies.”6 However, some have questioned
the official mission and this has led to speculation that its “real” mission may be secretly an offensive
weapons platform. The actual activities the X-37B has carried out indicate its mission is likely exactly what
the USSF claims it is.
Over the course of its eight missions, the X-37B has served as a testbed for multiple technologies,
including autonomous orbital landing, advanced Hall Effect thrusters,7 and oscillating heat pipes8 that
have been used operationally in other programs. The X-37B has also served as a research platform and
hosted experiments on material exposure and degradation, space radiation on seeds, and microwave
power beaming. Multiple cubesats have also deployed on X-37B missions, although it is not always clear
if they were deployed as rideshares from the upper stage or from the payload bay of the X-37B itself.
Finally, the X-37B’s orbit has typically been in line with that of an imagery, surveillance, or reconnaissance
(ISR) platform, such as repeating ground tracks and low altitudes. Given that the OTV-7 mission was at a
much higher altitude of nearly 39,000 km, it is possible it was testing a new type of sensor or payload, but
it is unclear what kind or why.

While there has been much speculation of the X-37B’s role for interacting with other satellites on orbit
or serving as a weapons platform, there is no evidence to date of this occurring. To date, the X-37B has
never approached or rendezvoused with any other known space object and generally orbits far below
the vast majority of operational satellites, with the exception of OTV-7, which was spotted by an amateur
astronomer in in a Highly Elliptical Orbit (HEO) of 323 x 38,838 km x 59.1 deg inclination.9
Physics further limits its utility as a space-to-ground weapon. The X-37B’s payload bay and available
power are very limited. Hyperkinetic weapons dropped from the bay would need to be equipped with
thrusters capable of performing a significant deorbit burn, which is unlikely given the available room. The
X-37B itself re-enters like the space shuttle landing at an estimated 200 mph (321 kph), which means it
travels in the atmosphere much slower than a nuclear re-entry vehicle on a ballistic arc or a hyperkinetic
weapon. It thus would need to carry conventional explosives to do any significant damage.10 The X-37B
after re-entry would be a slow moving, not-very-maneuverable glide bomb, easy prey for any air defense
system along its path to the target.
MAJOR ACTIVITIES OF X-37B
The first flight of the X-37B was in . OTV-1 was launched on a United Launch Alliance-built Atlas
V Evolved Expendable Launch Vehicle. The program was described as a “flexible space test platform to
conduct various experiments,” and that it would “allow satellite sensors, subsystems, components and
associated technology to be efficiently transported to and from the space environment.”11 It landed at
Vandenberg Air Force Base, California, in after 224 days in orbit.12 The second flight of
the X-37B, OTV-2 began in with a launch via the Atlas V rocket from Cape Canaveral Air Force
Station, Florida. Program officials again did not say anything specific about the mission but did anticipate
that it would be able to stay on orbit for 270 days, given analysis of OTV-1’s performance parameters.13
Around the 270-day mark of its flight, USAF officials announced that they were extending its mission to
allow for “additional experimentation opportunities.” It landed at Vandenberg AFB, California, in , after 469 days in orbit.14
The third flight of the X-37B, OTV-3 was the first to reuse a flight vehicle, the OTV-1 in this case. The
original launch was planned for , but due to a flight anomaly, the launch was pushed back.15
OTV-3 was launched in via an Atlas 5 from Cape Canaveral Air Force Base, Florida, with
a mission to “incorporate the lessons learned during the refurbishment process on OTV-1.” It spent 675
days in orbit before landing in , at Vandenberg AFB, California.16 In , OTV-4 was
launched via an Atlas 5 launch rocket from Cape Canaveral Air Force Station, Florida.17 Program officials
stated that OTV-4 would carry a Hall thruster to experiment with this technology for its eventual use in
the Advanced Extremely High Frequency (AEHF) military satellite program.18 NASA officials stated that
OTV-4 would carry Materials Exposure and Technology Innovation in Space (METIS), a program intended
to test almost 100 different types of materials to the space environment for over 200 days.19 OTV-4 landed
at NASA’s Space Shuttle Landing Site at the Kennedy Space Center, Florida, in , after 718 days
in space.20
In , OTV-5 launched from the Kennedy Space Center, Florida, via SpaceX’s Falcon 9
reusable launch vehicle. It was announced that the mission would carry “small satellite rideshare” and
an “Advanced Structurally Embedded Thermal Spreader payload to test experimental electronics and
oscillating heat pipe technologies in the long duration space environment.”21 On October 27, 2019, the
OTV-5 flight of the X-37B completed an, at that time, record-breaking 780-day stay in orbit with a landing
at NASA’s Kennedy Space Center Shuttle Landing Facility.22

OTV-6 featured a new service module added to the aft of the spacecraft which gave it more room for
payloads and experiments.23 One of these experiments was the satellite FalconSAT-8, built by students at
the USAF academy.24 OTV-6 also tested an on-orbit power beaming system (PRAM) which collected solar
power and transformed it into a microwave beam. Russian sources claim that in , OTV-6
released a small object that spent a day about 200 meters away from OTV-6 before moving away entirely.
OTV-6 landed in after spending 908 days in orbit, which is the longest flight to date for
the X-37B.25

MANEUVERABILITY OF THE X-37B
There has also been significant speculation over the maneuverability of the X-37B while on orbit. The
X-37B does have onboard thrusters that give it a large specific impulse, a measurement of a rocket
engine’s efficiency, and the X-37B has raised and lowered its orbit fairly often, particularly during OTV-
3, OTV-4, and OTV-5, suggesting significant delta-v capability as well. Former Secretary of the Air Force
Heather Wilson publicly suggested that the X-37B could also dip down into the atmosphere and use
its wings to make unpredictable changes to its orbit.32 In , General Chance Saltzman,
Chief of Space Operations, stated that the United States had announced it intended to use aerobraking
maneuvers because, “We wanted to let everybody know that we were going to do it…We want other
nations to observe it. We want to share the data.”33 Aerobraking maneuvers consist of a series of passes
through the Earth’s atmosphere to lower its orbit in a manner that uses less fuel, for the purpose of
getting low enough orbit to safely discard service module components. To complete the aforementioned
aerobraking maneuvers, the vehicle had to undergo modifications, including development of a new
collision avoidance system.34
TRANSPARENCY CONCERNS
Much of the speculation and consternation over the X-37B stems from the project’s secretive nature.
The mission and activities of the X-37B have long been classified, and the program’s budget is currently
unknown. The US military openly applauds the program for being a significant success, but does not
provide much information about its on-orbit activities. This may indicate it is part of a covert intelligence
program, but it may also indicate the testing of offensive technologies or capabilities. From the mission’s
launch to , the USSF had identified 1.7 million collision avoidance opportunities, though it
is unclear as to what that means.35
SECRET DEPLOYMENT OF SATELLITES
The X-37B secretly deployed three small satellites during its fifth mission between 2017 and 2019.
Although the USAF stated before launch that OTV-5 would deploy small satellites, it was unclear if
they were rideshares deployed from the upper stage or deployed from the X-37B payload bay itself.36
The actual deployment time of the three satellites was not announced and is still uncertain. The three
satellites were not included in the public satellite catalog maintained by the US military until after OTV-5
landed. The United States did not provide any orbital positional information on OTV-5 or the three small
satellites it deployed. The United States reported OTV-5 to the United Nations in conformity with the
Convention on Registration of Objects Launched into Outer Space on September 17, 2018, one year after
launch and on the same timeline as past X-37B flights.37 This withholding of information raises questions
about US commitment to international norms and transparency and confidence building measures, as
well as the purpose of these satellites.
