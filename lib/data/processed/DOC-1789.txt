WPS Women, peace and security

Introduction
This year marks 25 years since the United Nations Security Council Resolution
1325 on women, peace and security (WPS) stressed the link between gender
equality and international peace and security. The resolution underscored the
importance of the full and equal participation of women in all efforts towards
the maintenance and promotion of peace and security, including UN peace
operations. Following the resolution, organizations and member states involved
in multilateral peace operations affirmed their commitment to increase women’s
participation in these activities. Increasing the number of uniformed and civilian
women at all levels has since become a priority of the UN Secretariat. The
Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) Secretariat and,
more recently, the European External Action Service (EEAS) of the European
Union (EU) have followed suit and developed strategies establishing specific
targets and deadlines to enhance women’s participation in their respective
personnel. In 2025, 25 years after the adoption of the resolution, some progress
has been achieved in enhancing women’s representation in multilateral peace
operations, but much remains to be done.
This overview of women’s representation in multilateral peace operations
presents data on aggregate personnel trends, annual snapshots of data on
women’s representation in leadership, and annual averages of women in the
personnel of peace operations and in member state contributions. It looks at
UN peace operations (i.e. both UN peacekeeping operations and UN special
political missions), EU Common Security and Defence Policy (CSDP) missions
and operations, and OSCE field operations. The objective is to support the
efforts of multilateral organizations and their member states to increase the
representation of women in multilateral peace operations.
The statistics presented here aim to inform and foster the debate on the future of
the women and peace and security agenda within the context of multilateral
peace operations. They illustrate to what extent the organizations
deploying multilateral peace operations and their member states are
making progress towards increasing women’s representation in
multilateral peace operations. Having a clearer picture of the status
of women’s participation in multilateral peace operations and
keeping track of progress also help in recognizing and targeting
potential shortcomings. Moreover, this overview enhances the
transparency of multilateral peace operations and provides
insights into their recent trends and developments.

Women in the UN military
The UN’s 2018 Uniformed Gender Parity Strategy established the goal of reaching 25 per cent women among
military experts and staff officers and 15 per cent among troops in UN peace operations by 2028. Under the Action
for Peacekeeping (A4P) Declaration of Shared Commitments, the UN advocated for increasing the number of women
in peacekeeping across all levels and key positions. Subsequently, Action for Peacekeeping Plus (A4P+) emphasized
the need to enhance the meaningful participation of women in these roles. The proportion of women among
military experts and staff officers is nearing the strategy’s target and has surpassed the intermediary 2025 target
of 22 per cent. At 8 per cent, however, the proportion of women among troops remains significantly below both the
2025 intermediary target (12 per cent) and the 2028 goal.

Women in the UN police
The UN’s 2018 Uniformed Gender Parity Strategy established the target of 20 per cent women in formed police units
and 30 per cent women among individual police officers by 2028. The A4P initiative reaffirmed the commitments
of the UN and its member states. In 2021, the UN reached the 30 per cent goal for the proportion of women
among individual police officers. In 2025, this proportion was 31 per cent, the same as in the previous two years.
The proportion of women in formed police units increased in 2025, surpassing this year’s target of 15 per cent and
reaching 18 per cent.

Women in UN civilian personnel
The UN’s 2017 System-wide Strategy on Gender Parity set targets for the equal representation of women and men
in several areas, including among civilian personnel in UN peace operations. The strategy allows for variation within
a 47–53 per cent range as an indication of such parity. In , the Department of Peace Operations issued a
new Gender-responsive UN Peacekeeping Operations Policy. It builds on previous strategies and efforts by reaffirming
the need to promote both civilian and uniformed gender parity across all levels and roles, including by creating an
enabling environment for the full, equal and meaningful participation of women deployed in peacekeeping operations.
However, the goal of achieving gender parity in civilian personnel remains distant for most UN peace operations.
In 2025, only UNSCOL had at least 47 per cent women in its civilian personnel.

Women in OSCE field operations
The OSCE Secretariat’s Gender Parity Strategy for 2019–26 set the goal of gender parity across all staff categories
by 2026, including in OSCE field operations, allowing for variation within a 45–55 per cent range. The OSCE 2025
Annual Progress Report on Gender Equality reconfirmed the strategy and its target and pointed to gains in women’s
representation, particularly at senior levels in contracted positions. Nevertheless, the OSCE continued to observe
low nomination rates of women to senior positions among its member states. Five out of 12 operations had at least
45 per cent women in their personnel in 2025: the OSCE Centre in Ashgabat, OMIK, OM Moldova, OM Serbia and
the OSCE Project Co-ordinator in Uzbekistan.

Women in EU CSDP missions and operations
In 2023, EU member states renewed the Civilian CSDP Compact to strengthen the EU’s civilian crisis management
missions, committing among other things to increasing women’s representation in line with UN Security Council
Resolution 1325. The renewal set the concrete objective of reaching at least 40 per cent women in EU CSDP
missions by 2025, with a longer-term goal of parity at all levels—something the previous compact lacked. To support
implementation, the EEAS updated the 2021 Strategy and Action Plan, completing the process at the end of 2024.
The Strategy and Action Plan to Enhance Women’s Participation in Civilian CSDP Missions 2024–27 is now in
place. Nevertheless, progress has been slow and by mid 2025, only 1 of the EU’s 12 civilian missions had met the
40 per cent target: EUBAM Rafah.

Concluding remarks
Since the UN Security Council passed its landmark resolution on women, peace
and security 25 years ago, organizations and member states have pledged and
set targets to improve the representation of women in peace operations. There
has been some progress in this regard, but it remains modest and gender parity
is still a distant goal. Overall, women continue to be under-represented in the
field.
For UN military personnel, both the overall number and the proportion of
women have increased. Despite steady gains over the past decade, women’s
representation among military experts and staff officers decreased slightly in
2025, although it remained above the year’s intermediate target and on the path
towards the 2028 goal of 25 per cent. By contrast, while higher than last year,
women’s representation in troop contingents was still low, making the target of
15 per cent by 2028 a challenge at the current pace.
In comparison, women’s participation in the UN police is better aligned with
the Uniformed Gender Parity Strategy’s 2028 goals. In 2021, the category of
individual police officers met the 2028 target of at least 30 per cent women,
and the proportion has continued to hover at around 30–32 per cent. In formed
police units, women’s participation surpassed the 2025 target of 15 per cent
and has increased steadily since 2021.
Although the proportion of women in UN civilian personnel is higher than in
the military and police categories, women still remain a minority across all the
UN peace operations and field offices analysed, except UNSCOL. UNSCOL was
the only case to meet the UN System-wide Strategy on Gender Parity target of
47–53 per cent women in civilian staff. In 2025, the proportion of women in
UN civilian personnel was relatively stable across peace operations and special
political missions, with slight increases in both.
Five of the 12 OSCE field operations met the target of 45–55 per cent women
in their personnel in 2025: the OSCE Centre in Ashgabat, OMIK, OM Moldova,
OM Serbia and the OSCE Project Co-ordinator in Uzbekistan. This was 4 more
operations than in the previous year. After a few years in which the proportion
of women in OSCE field operations was relatively stable, in 2025 it increased by
7.8 percentage points and reached 45 per cent. The number of women in these
operations also increased.
Over the latest reporting periods, there were decreases in the number of women
in civilian EU CSDP missions (to ) and in military EU CSDP missions
and operations (to ), partly due to the closures of EUCAP Sahel
Niger and EUTM Mali respectively. However, the proportion of women increased
in military missions and operations and remained unchanged in civilian
missions. Of the 19 missions and operations, only EUBAM Rafah reached at
least 40 per cent women in its personnel, consistent with the target set by

the renewed Civilian CSDP Compact. Regarding women seconded to EU CSDP
missions, 9 contributing countries met or exceeded the 40 per cent target for
their seconded personnel: Belgium, Canada, Croatia, Cyprus, Estonia, Finland,
Lithuania, Norway and Sweden.
The 2025 overview of women’s participation in leadership roles across peace
operations shows a mixed picture. Participation increased markedly in the OSCE,
up by 17 percentage points to 25 per cent, and in the UN military, which went
from having no woman force commander in 2024 to 1 woman in 2025 (out
of 8 force commanders). By contrast, the UN police saw a noticeable decline,
from 40 per cent in 2024 to 20 per cent in 2025. For UN civilian and EU CSDP
missions and operations, the proportions were unchanged from last year, at
30 per cent and 15 per cent respectively. The highest proportion of women in
leadership roles was observed in UN civilian personnel.
This annual overview of women’s representation in peace operations is important
for understanding the state of play, particularly related to the strategies and
targets set by organizations deploying peace operations. However, while the
numbers are important for fostering discussions on women’s participation, they
have limitations and do not address the deeper challenges. A narrow focus on
headcounts can also encourage compliance with targets without addressing
underlying constraints in recruitment pipelines, retention, promotion, training
and workplace safety. It can also create situations where women are present in
numbers but lack equal opportunities or influence.
Alongside tracking representation, organizations need to consider indicators
that capture the influence and quality of participation. In line with the WPS
agenda, this focus on participation should advance together with the
other WPS pillars of prevention, protection, and relief and recovery.
They should work as mutually reinforcing elements in the integrated
implementation of the agenda. Finally, as part of this integrated
effort, it is crucial to simultaneously highlight the need for
institutional measures that create enabling conditions for the
meaningful and equitable participation of women in peace
operations.
