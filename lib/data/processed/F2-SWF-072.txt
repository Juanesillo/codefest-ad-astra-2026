DA-ASAT PROGRAMS
DA-ASATs use a ground, air, or sea-launched rocket to place a kinetic kill vehicle (KKV) on a ballistic
trajectory up into space. After separation from the rocket, the KKV uses onboard guidance, navigation,
and control systems to identify and track a targeted space object and fine-tune its trajectory to create
a hypervelocity collision. DA-ASATs are very similar to midcourse missile defense interceptors, with the
difference being the missile defense targets are also on ballistic trajectories. Unlike a co-orbital ASAT, the
DA-ASAT KKV itself does not have enough velocity to achieve
orbit and any resulting fragments are likewise unlikely to
remain in orbit unless they were part of an orbital object that
was struck.
Indian DA-ASAT Development
Following the Chinese 2007 DA-ASAT weapon test, many
Indian officials worried about what it meant for the
militarization of space. This, combined with ongoing border
disputes and concerns about China’s role in the Asia-Pacific,
led many leaders to begin considering whether India
should have its own ASAT capabilities.¹ Dr. K. Kasturirangan,
former head of the Indian Space Research Organisation
(ISRO), said in that “India has spent a huge
sum to develop its capabilities and place these assets in
space. Hence, it becomes necessary to protect them from
adversaries. There is a need to look at means of securing
these.”² Over the ensuing decade, Indian officials adjusted
their take on developing and testing ASAT technology from
simply ground simulation to needing to have “all the building
blocks...in place” as V.K. Saraswat, who was then the head
of India’s Defence Research and Development Organisation
(DRDO), stated.³ At the time, he said they had “no plans for

In 2017, India began a series of interceptor tests against suborbital targets with the Prithvi Air Defense
(PAD) system (later to be replaced by the Prithvi Defense Vehicle, or PDV) and the Advanced Area Defense
(AAD) system.
The PDV was successfully test-fired in , when it was reported to have intercepted its
target at 97 km in altitude, and is intended to provide exo-atmospheric intercepts.⁵ It was tested again
in , this time at night, and was able to “successfully engage” its target.⁶ The AAD was
launched in to make a successful intercept at an altitude of 15–25 km⁷ and was later tested
in , successfully destroying its target, which was surrounded by decoys.⁸ In ,
government officials stated that the system was complete.9
The AD-1 missile was successfully flown for the first time in a test and was designed to
be able to provide both endo- and exo-atmospheric intercepts.10 It flew for the second time in 
when a target missile was launched as part of an overall test of the system’s ability to track, guide, and
communicate; an intercept was not attempted. This was for India’s planned Phase-II Ballistic Missile
Defence System, which is intended to intercept medium- to long-range ballistic missiles.11 Phase III is
intended to defend against hypersonic threats and MIRV’d ballistic missiles; DRDO is reported to be
developing two new interceptors, AD-AH and AD-AM, to do so.12
On February 12, 2019, India attempted to launch and test their first ASAT weapon against Microsat-R,
one of India’s military imaging satellites launched previously that year.13 The solid-fueled PDV failed
thirty seconds into flight. At the time, India gave a vague announcement that they would be conducting
a missile test and it was not clear at that point that the test was of ASAT capabilities. It was not until the
successful test later in March that the outside observers noted the same trajectory and target satellite
passing overhead for both the failed and successful tests.
On March 27, 2019, the Indian Prime Minister announced that they had successfully conducted Mission
Shakti, where a PDV MK-II interceptor successfully destroyed a satellite at an altitude of about 300 km.14
The missile used was from India’s indigenously developed missile defense system, a PDV MK-II, and the
satellite target was Microsat-R, a medium-sized (740 kg) Indian military imaging satellite launched into a
low Sun-synchronous orbit in . ISRO had launched the satellite but did not know that it was
intended to be an ASAT target, just that it was intended to have a defense application.15 The kill vehicle’s
terminal guidance used a ring laser gyro-based inertial navigation system and a strap-down Imaging
Infrared Seeker; the interception was done at a speed of 10 km/second, with the electro optical tracking
system tracking the entire engagement.16 Reportedly, the decision was made in 2017 to undertake the
test (initially called Project XSV-1), giving DRDO engineers about 30 months to ensure that the kill vehicle
was ready for it.17

In a fact sheet released about the ASAT test, the Indian government explained, “The test was done
to verify that India has the capability to safeguard our space assets. It is the Government of India’s
responsibility to defend the country’s interests in outer space,” but went on to say, “We are against the
weaponisation of Outer Space and support international efforts to reinforce the safety and security of
space-based assets.”18 After the test, DRDO chair G. Sateesh Reddy stated, “We don’t need any more tests
in this orbit now,” but did not rule out tests at higher orbits.19
Indian officials downplayed concerns about large amounts of debris being created by this test, stating
that the intercept was at a low enough altitude that most of the debris would reenter in a few days,
with the entirety of it coming back down within 45 days at most.20 However, the intercept created 130
pieces of trackable debris with some pieces thrown to an altitude of more than 1000 km due to collision
dynamics.21 The debris ended up taking over three years to de-orbit. While there was some international
outcry over the further weaponization of space and the creation of debris in highly trafficked orbits
such as that of the ISS, due to the overall smaller amount of debris than previous destructive tests many
countries remained silent.
A prime motivation for the test was likely to ensure India would be grandfathered into any future
ban on DA-ASAT testing. Successfully demonstrating their own DA-ASAT capability might have been a
political prerequisite for India to support discussions on a future ban and showcase their strength as a
new military space power. While it is possible that Indian officials may decide to test another ASAT, this
capability is more likely to be useful as a bargaining chip or a way to demonstrate that India is keeping
pace with China.
